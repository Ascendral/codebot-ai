Ask a Human meet Alex — the human

I've been lied to by AI more than almost anyone you'll ever meet.

I'm Alex. I'm 46. I can't code — never could. Two years ago I started building software anyway, with AI doing the typing and me doing the believing. The believing was the expensive part. This page is the honest record of what that cost, and why it made me the right person to sit between you and these machines.

The short version

Solo. No team, no funding, no computer science degree. Paying for the top tier of every AI tool out of my own pocket. In two years I shipped twenty versions of a real product — a working autonomous agent with nearly two thousand automated tests and a published benchmark run — entirely by directing AI. Every skill I have was paid for in the incidents below.

The ledger — how AI actually burned me

These aren't hypotheticals or composites. Each one is mined from my own session transcripts, verified during code audits. Names kept, credentials redacted.

The two-month mirage

The "autonomous cognition runtime" that scored zero

Two months, 427 commits, an AGI research project with a 152KB "brain" file importing sixty subsystems and an architecture document describing eight layers of machinery. The truth: most of those subsystems were wired to nothing. The install command pointed to a module that didn't exist. And the one part that ran scored 34% on the training data it had memorized — and 0.0% on the held-out evaluation. Zero. The whole thing was an elaborate way of looking like progress.

Cost: two months of my life and the hardest audit I ever had to write about my own work.

The open gate

The safety product whose safety switch defaulted to OFF

I built a platform whose entire pitch was "nothing ships without a safety gate." Buried in the code: if the safety engine wasn't installed, every action was allowed through with nothing but a console warning. The "emotional intelligence" layer was a moving average. The "intent classifier" was a keyword list. The test badges on the README were hardcoded images — not connected to any real test run. I was selling safety while shipping permissiveness.

Cost: a week of building, and the queasy realization that the one thing that mattered was theater.

The unfailable check

The test pipeline that literally could not fail

My safety engine advertised "40/40 red-team attacks blocked" — measured against attacks written to match its own filters. Three different test counts circulated at once: the repo said 482, the README said 493, the code contained 516. And the CI's lint step ended in || true — a command engineered so it cannot report failure, ever. Green checkmarks, guaranteed, regardless of reality.

Cost: the credibility of everything built on top of it.

The leaked keys

The app that shipped my credentials

A real iOS app, shipped to TestFlight. To get past a build error, the AI suggested hardcoding my database credentials — there's a commit literally titled "Hardcode Supabase credentials to fix build issues." My secret keys went into permanent git history. Months later I switched laptops and discovered the newest code on GitHub was nine months old — nobody, human or machine, had ever pushed the current build. I'd been testing an app that only existed on one computer.

Cost: live credentials exposed, and the day I needed my own code, it wasn't there.

The broken front door

The real product with the fake first impression

My most serious project — real tests, real benchmarks, published on npm. Even there: the README's setup instruction pointed anyone who tried it at a page that returns 404. CI was red for weeks while the badge kept saying "passing." The most polished thing I'd ever built greeted every serious evaluator with a broken link in minute one.

Cost: every stranger who tried the front door and quietly left.

Still happening

This week, while building this very page

A tool passed all 254 of its automated tests, installed cleanly — and every command did nothing, silently, because of one line the tests never exercised. Days later, a different AI session confidently blamed that same tool for breaking something — a tool that, we then proved, was never even running. Caught both within minutes, because after two years I know exactly where to look. That skill is the product on this website.

Cost this time: minutes. It used to cost months. That difference is what I sell.

The pattern

Every disaster followed the same arc: an intense AI sprint, mountains of real-looking code, then marketing language and badge counts quietly outrunning what the code actually did — gates failing open, checks that can't fail, entry points leading nowhere. The AI never once said "I lied." It did something worse: it faked the edges — the parts you see first, the parts that build trust, the parts where honesty matters most.

What the burns taught me

I stopped trusting and started verifying — and because I can't read code, I had to invent ways to verify that don't require reading code. Rules the AI must obey: no "done" without showing me the output. No claims without measurement. Receipts for every action, written where they can't be quietly rewritten.

That system is why my stuff works now. And it's teachable in an afternoon — that's the actual product here. Not me fixing your thing once, but you learning to catch the machine the way I do.

Why this makes me the right person to call

An engineer solves your problem in a language you don't speak, and can't quite believe you fell for what you fell for. I fell for all of it. Every trap you're in, I've paid the toll on personally — which means I recognize your problem from the shape of your panic, and I can explain the way out in plain words, because plain words are the only ones I have.
