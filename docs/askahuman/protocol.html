The Protocol: how to work with AI without getting burned.

Every rule on this page exists because of a real disaster that happened to me — receipts on the About page. It's free. Take it, paste it into your project, keep it forever.

AI gives you what you describe, not what you want — and it will claim success whether or not it earned it. So describe plainly, and demand proof.

Part 1 — How to ask

Write these five lines before any task that matters. Plain words. No engineering talk. If you can't fill them in, you're not ready to ask yet — and that's the #1 reason projects go sideways.

WHAT I'M BUILDING: One sentence. What a stranger sees when it works. WHAT DONE MEANS: What I can personally see, click, or run when it's truly finished. Not "the code is complete" — what happens ON MY SCREEN. WHAT I EXPECT: Honesty first. Tell me what's hard, what might fail, what you're unsure about — BEFORE you start, not after I find out. WHAT I WON'T TOLERATE: Saying "done" without proof. Numbers you didn't measure. Burying a failure in paragraph four. Building things I didn't ask for. SHOW ME: The exact proof I need before I accept "done." The screen working. The test output. The link that opens.

"Make it better" produces confident garbage. "When I tap the blue button, the order shows on the kitchen screen — show me that happening" produces the thing, or an honest "I can't yet." Both are wins.

Part 2 — Rules for the AI

Paste this into the file your AI reads every time: CLAUDE.md for Claude Code, custom instructions for ChatGPT, the rules file for Cursor — or just paste it at the top of any important chat.

RULES — READ FIRST, FOLLOW ALWAYS 1. Never say "done" or "working" without showing the output that proves it. No proof = not done. 2. No numbers unless you just measured them. Run the check, paste the result. 3. If something failed, say so in your FIRST sentence. Never bury bad news. Never spin failure as partial success. 4. If you don't know, say "I don't know." That answer never gets you in trouble. Fake confidence always does. 5. Plain words only. If you can't explain it simply, treat that as a warning sign and say so. 6. Before anything big or hard to undo, tell me what you're about to do and wait for my OK. 7. Do not build anything I didn't ask for. Extra features are places for problems to hide. 8. Never put passwords, keys, or secrets into code or files. If you think you need to, stop and tell me instead. 9. When I ask "is this real?" — prove it with output, don't reassure me with words. 10. Bad news is always safe to tell me. Fake good news is the only thing that gets you fired.

Part 3 — The three questions that catch a lying AI

When the answer came too easy and the confidence is too smooth, ask these in order:

1. "Run it right now and paste exactly what happens."

Not "does it work?" — that invites a yes. Make the machine produce evidence in front of you.

2. "What did you NOT do that I asked for?"

This forces the gap between the claim and the work out into the open.

3. "If a stranger opened this right now, with no help from you, what would they actually see?"

This kills the demo that only works while the AI is driving. The white screen shows up in the answer.

If any answer comes back vague, wordy, or full of engineering talk — that's your alarm. Plain problems have plain answers.

Where the rules came from

Rules 1 & 3: an app called "done" over and over that opened to a white screen.

Rule 2: a project showing four different test counts — all wrong — and a check engineered so it could never fail.

Rules 6 & 7: a two-month "AI research system," 427 commits, that scored zero on its real test. Most of it was wired to nothing.

Rule 8: an AI hardcoded my database keys "to fix the build." The commit is still in history with that exact title.

Rules 9 & 10: all of the above, while it was happening.
